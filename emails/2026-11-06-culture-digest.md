# Culture Digest — 6 November 2026

Randall Dunn's Philip K. Dick-inspired "speculative music" and Willi Carlisle's Tyler Childers-produced double album lead the listening, Charles Ray's $3 million sculptures get a hard look, Edmund Richardson's Alexander takes the Baillie Gifford, and James Gray sends Adam Driver to 1980s Queens.

## 🎵 Music
- **Randall Dunn — *Manifold of Partially Actualized Realities*** — Out today on Kou Records, and The Quietus's Album of the Week: the producer behind Sunn O))) and Earth records leaves drone behind for a kosmische, cinematic palette of strings, winds, berimbau, saxophone and Moog, built around Philip K. Dick's 1974 "pink light" vision, with spoken-word pieces read by Jim Jarmusch. Aydin Khalili hears "a work of speculative music" from someone with "an unusual ability to bring disparate sounds into close proximity without smoothing away their differences". Start with the opener, "The Ultimate and Universal Ash".

- **Willi Carlisle — *The Universal Bubba*** — Out today on Signature Sounds: the Arkansas "big tent folksinger" goes big with a 17-track double album produced by Tyler Childers and his band the Food Stamps, mixing old-time, Cajun, funk and stranger things; Carlisle says it "should sound like getting drunk at the Civil War reenactment". Stereogum made it Album of the Week, Chris DeVille's verdict being simply "It works like it's supposed to." Start with "Mason Jar at the Center of the World".
  Listen: [The Universal Bubba on Spotify](https://open.spotify.com/album/12nyB0HBhKLnGGzSkckxL1)

- **Steven Wilson — *Requiem for a Village*** — Out today: a folk-horror concept album set in an unnamed English village, co-written with XTC's Andy Partridge, who wrote the narration performed by David Tennant. Wilson used only analogue synths and left the imperfections in, citing *Children of the Stones*, *The Wicker Man* and *The Prisoner*: "the nostalgia, the question of whether the village really exists, and does it really matter?" Try "On Chapel Slope".
  Read/Watch: [Dave Everley's Louder feature on the album and the Tennant casting](https://www.loudersound.com/bands-artists/steven-wilson-david-tennant-new-album)

He calls it "anti-ambient", the answer to his own question "What might the most euphoric possible variant of ambient sound like?" Start with "Nokik Messiah".
  Listen: [Music for Empty Stadiums on Spotify](https://open.spotify.com/album/5TrIb5RT795Fuwx2Gqnv1U)

Nick Roseblade gives it 8/10 in Clash: "His albums are meticulous. He doesn't release anything until it's right." Try "I Surrender", or the opener "Precious" for the gospel side.

## 🎨 Art
- **Has time been unkind to Charles Ray?** — Mitch Speed uses a Kassel survey to ask why Ray's hyper-crafted sculptures (*Boy with Frog*, *Aluminum Girl*, *Mountain Lion Attacking a Dog*) now read as reactionary rather than knowing: luxury money ("An original Ray will set you back a chilly $3 million"), machine-made surfaces that land differently in the age of AI imitation, and a drift from early wit to spectacle. "The necessary alliance of this profoundly expensive art with the gilded class flattens satiric distance." A critic arguing with work he once found magical.
  Read/Watch: [Mitch Speed, "Has Time Been Unkind to Charles Ray's Sculptures?", ArtReview](https://artreview.com/has-time-been-unkind-to-charles-rays-sculptures-fridericianum-kassel-feature-mitch-speed/)
- **George Shaw's dead robin** — Keith Miller's close reading of a single picture, *Ex National Treasure* (2026), a small enamel painting of a dead robin from Shaw's "Albion Groans" series (the title is Blake's). Miller reads it as "allegorical rather than literal, charged with a Blakean spirit of revelation", sets it beside Zurbarán's *Agnus Dei*, and praises "its quiet, insistent presence, its smallness and sadness", noting that Shaw's enamel handling "is notably more assured than many contemporary artists' use of oils".
  Read/Watch: [Keith Miller, "The quiet intensity of George Shaw's painting", Apollo](https://apollo-magazine.com/george-shaw-painting-ex-national-treasure-robin/)
- **Doris Salcedo on Colombia's emptied museum** — Last month Colombia's president ordered dozens of objects (LGBTQ+ and feminist material, 2016 peace-deal records, items from two assassinated left-wing leaders) pulled from the Museo Nacional in Bogotá; a court has ordered them back, citizens have hung copies at the entrance. Valentina Di Liscia's long interview with the sculptor of absence finds her reading the empty wall spaces as the most eloquent thing in the building: "those absences become an extremely powerful presence." Her opening line: "Works of art last much longer than their governments." Free registration needed to read in full.
  Read/Watch: [Valentina Di Liscia's Hyperallergic interview with Doris Salcedo](https://hyperallergic.com/doris-salcedo-on-colombias-refusal-to-forget/)
- **Matisse's *The Red Studio*, explained** — A sofa watch: James Payne's Great Art Explained episode traces how the 1911 painting flattened the picture plane and stripped out the usual cues of depth, failed to sell even at the 1913 Armory Show, was hidden by Matisse for a decade and hung in a London nightclub before MoMA canonised it. Kate Mothes's accompanying note: "It took decades for the unorthodox work to be recognized not as repulsive but revolutionary."
  Read/Watch: [Kate Mothes on the Great Art Explained film, Colossal (video embedded)](https://www.thisiscolossal.com/2026/11/great-art-explained-henri-matisse-the-red-studio-video/)

## 📚 Books & Writing
- **Edmund Richardson — *Alexander: God, King, Man*** — Winner of the Baillie Gifford Prize on Wednesday, an upset over Patrick Radden Keefe and the first ancient history to win since the prize began. Richardson follows Alexander from debt-ridden 20-year-old Macedonian king to living god and death at 32 in Babylon, drawing on sources in a dozen ancient languages; Andrew Lynch calls this "richly detailed and full-blooded portrait" one of the most enjoyable Alexanders yet, and the judges called it "blockbuster history with page-turning narrative pull". Harari and Snyder territory, with more battles.
  Read/Watch: [Andrew Lynch's Irish Times review](https://www.irishtimes.com/culture/books/review/2026/08/22/alexander-god-king-man/) (paywall)
- **Louise Kennedy — *Stations*** — Out in the US this week from Riverhead (Bloomsbury in the UK): the follow-up to *Trespasses*, in which Róisín and Red meet at 16 in a Kildare town in 1982, flee to a London squat, lose touch and find each other again over 25 years of the Troubles, addiction and Irish economic despair. The Guardian called it "a blisteringly good second novel"; in Sophie Lee's long interview Kennedy explains the novel's governing belief: "I think fundamentally we don't change at all."
  Read/Watch: [Sophie Lee's interview with Louise Kennedy, Cultured](https://culturedmag.com/article/2026/11/02/literature-louise-kennedy-stations-novel-interview/)
- **Tom Drury — *West of Loveland*** — Out 3 Nov from Grove (Hodder in the UK, February): the fourth and farewell Grouse County novel, in which 15-year-old Rocky and his aunt Helen, a speaker builder, find a revolver by the road and Sheriff Dan Norman's slow inquiry threads through the whole town. Carver country, Iowa branch. Michael Magras calls it "a perceptive, unsentimental portrait of middle American life"; Kirkus, "a fond, brilliant farewell to Drury's slice of the uncanny Midwest".
  Read/Watch: [Michael Magras's Shelf Awareness review](https://shelf-awareness.com/theshelf/2026-08-14/west_of_loveland.html)
- **David Kynaston — *Deep into the Sixties: Britain 1965-66*** — The sixth volume of *Tales of a New Jerusalem*, 704 pages from October 1965 to the World Cup, taking in divorce, abortion and homosexuality law reform, the National Plan and the redevelopment of the cities, all told through diaries and letters. Gordon Brown's review calls the series "masterful and highly addictive" and quotes Kynaston's northern witness: "The Sixties never came to Blackburn."
  Read/Watch: [Gordon Brown, "The decade that changed everything", New Statesman](https://newstatesman.com/culture/books/book-of-the-day/2026/11/the-decade-that-changed-everything) (paywall)
- **William T. Vollmann — *A Table for Fortune*** — Four volumes and about 3,000 pages from Arcade: CIA analyst Elliott Stevens from the end of the Cold War through 9/11, and his son Matthew, homeless by choice, searching for the real America. Nick Holdstock's Guardian review this week calls it "a hugely ambitious 3,000-page portrait of America's decline" and finds inside it "a dark workplace comedy of minor resentments, sexual tension and office banter"; Paul Wilner in Alta thinks it "may well be Vollmann's finest". For a long winter.
  Read/Watch: [Nick Holdstock's Guardian review](https://www.theguardian.com/books/2026/nov/04/a-table-for-fortune-by-william-t-vollmann-review-a-hugely-ambitious-3000-page-portrait-of-americas-decline)
