## 🎨 Art
- **Jonathan Jones v Bloomsbury** — Tate Britain's first Vanessa Bell and Duncan Grant survey since 1999 opened on Wednesday, and Jones's review is the week's most argumentative piece of art writing: the pair were "desperately mediocre modernists" who borrowed from Paris without ever committing to it, and the lives, the loves and the Cézannes and Picassos they owned are more interesting than their own pictures. "They try on avant garde ideas but always come back to tradition"; "biography can't set art alight." The argument is about the paintings themselves, so you can test it against the Tate's images from the sofa.
  Read/Watch: [Jonathan Jones, "A flaccid roam through Bloomsbury's desperately mediocre modernists", The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/artanddesign/2026/nov/09/vanessa-bell-duncan-grant-review-bloomsbury-mediocre-modernists)
- **Vermeer's vanishing act** — Susan Tallman's long survey of six new Vermeer books (Andrew Graham-Dixon's biography, Ruth Bernard Yeazell's *Vermeer's Afterlives*, Philip Steadman on the camera obscura and the Rijksmuseum's research volume among them) asks why so many smart writers insist on solving a painter whose withholding is the point. She is hardest on Graham-Dixon's theory that Vermeer was a Collegiant, a tower of suppositions, and ends where the paintings do: "His absence isn't a void awaiting meaning but a form of meaning in itself."
  Read/Watch: [Susan Tallman, "Vermeer's Vanishing Act", New York Review of Books](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/2026/11/05/vermeers-vanishing-act-johannes-vermeer/) (paywall)
- **The Jewish sisters in Renoir's *Pink and Blue*** — Euridice Arratia reviews Catherine Ostler's *The Renoir Girls*, which uses the 1881 double portrait of Alice and Elisabeth Cahen d'Anvers as a way into the Jewish banking family that commissioned it: the salon world, assimilation into the Catholic aristocracy, the Dreyfus Affair and the deportations that killed four members of the family, with a side-plot following *La Petite Irène*, looted by the Nazis and later bought by a Swiss arms dealer. A "duet of sugared-almond-colored sweetness" that opens onto "devastation and betrayal amid one of the darkest chapters of European history".
  Read/Watch: [Euridice Arratia, "The Jewish Sisters in Renoir's 'Pink and Blue'", Hyperallergic](https://hyperallergic.com/the-jewish-sisters-in-renoirs-pink-and-blue/)
- **Barry McGee, the geezer of graffiti** — Jonathan Griffin's profile of the 60-year-old San Francisco graffiti artist, now an elder statesman using a Jeffrey Deitch show in Los Angeles to lift other artists, as he once did with his "99CENT" discount-store pop-up, while the culture around him has drifted from refusing commerce to brand collaborations. "I became an old person, is what happened." And on how style spreads: "In graffiti, if someone cuts a hole in the fence, everybody goes through that same hole."
  Read/Watch: [Jonathan Griffin, "The Geezer of Graffiti Art, Barry McGee, Goes 'Another Round' in Los Angeles", New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/09/arts/design/the-geezer-of-graffiti-art-barry-mcgee-goes-another-round-in-los-angeles.html) (paywall)

## 📚 Books & Writing
- **M John Harrison — *The End of Everything*** — Monday's Booker winner, and a first on two counts: the first science fiction novel to take the prize, and at 81 Harrison its oldest winner. A sardonic post-apocalyptic satire set among the art towns of the English east coast, where "a sense of imminent collapse has become ingrained in everyday existence". Mary Beard said it "turns you upside down, raises a wry smile" and "leaves you with a twinge of optimism"; Olivia Ho in Literary Review called it "a work of uncanny beauty, tender and terrifying in equal parts". Harrison, on winning: "I feel like a proper writer at last, totally lacking in confidence." Serpent's Tail.
  Read/Watch: [Olivia Ho, "Close Encounters", Literary Review](https://literaryreview.co.uk/close-encounters)
- **Albert Camus and Maria Casarès — *Letters 1944–1959*** — The first full English translation (Penguin Classics, 1,200 pages, translated by Sandra Smith and Cory Stockwell) is the lead review in this month's Literary Review: 865 letters between Camus and the actress Maria Casarès, covering love, books, the Occupation, illness and mortality, and ending just before his death. Joanna Kavenna: "What an amazing behemoth of a book!"; the letters are "passionate, voluminous, at times torrential", and reading them "feels a bit like reading an extraordinary epistolary novel". For *The Stranger* reader who wants the man.
  Read/Watch: [Joanna Kavenna, "Here in the World", Literary Review](https://literaryreview.co.uk/here-in-the-world)
- **Harriet Clark — *The Hill*** — A debut out this month from Sceptre: Suzanna is eight when the novel opens, raised by her Manhattan grandparents while her mother Helen, a revolutionary who drove the getaway car in a failed bank robbery, serves life in the women's prison everyone calls "the hill". Susie Mesure in The Spectator calls it "a haunting bildungsroman that will test your prejudices about incarceration", and notes that Clark "is more interested in exploring concepts about what it means to be free" than in prison drama.
  Read/Watch: [Susie Mesure, "Sins of the mother", The Spectator](https://www.spectator.com.au/2026/11/sins-of-the-mother-the-hill-by-harriet-clark-reviewed/) (paywall)
- **Steven Strogatz and Alex Townsend — *Big Math: The Hidden Codes That Run Our World*** — Out 10 Nov from Little, Brown: two Cornell mathematicians trace the mathematics behind planetary motion, Netflix recommendations, Google Maps, Amazon logistics and AI models, and argue that some modern tools are black boxes beyond human intuition, so humans must "remain the conscience behind the code". Kirkus: "The authors write with clarity and charm"; "an engaging, cautionary tale of how mathematical concepts became essential tools for managing modern life". Meadows and Capra territory.
  Read/Watch: [Kirkus review](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/steven-strogatz/big-math/print/)
- **Andrew Rankin — *Yakuza: A 400-Year History of the Japanese Underworld*** — Out this month from Elliott & Thompson: Japan's criminal syndicates from the Edo period to the present, taking in the anti-gang laws, the shrinking membership and the 2022 disbanding of Tokyo's Anegasaki-kai. Tim Hornyak in Literary Review: "In the Western mind, Japan is often reduced to a set of stereotypes, of which one is the yakuza", yet they "continue to command a fascination far beyond their dwindling numbers". Crime history for the Ellroy and Celestin shelf.
  Read/Watch: [Tim Hornyak, "Four Fingers & Fading", Literary Review](https://literaryreview.co.uk/four-fingers-fading)

## 🎬 Film & TV
- **You Can See Everything (Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim)** — A24's Elizabeth Holmes documentary, filmed over three years with Holmes and her partner Billy Evans before and during her prison sentence, and the most startling non-fiction film of the year since Telluride. Adam Nayman finds it "absorbing and entertaining and occasionally beautiful", "what it looks like when filmmakers reflect on their practice", and also "a torture chamber equipped with ropes for hanging and knives for twisting into the ribs". UK cinemas from 20 Nov; in US cinemas since October.
  Read/Watch: [Adam Nayman's review for The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2026/10/16/movies/you-can-see-everything-review-nathan-fielder-elizabeth-holmes-documentary)
- **La Bola Negra / The Black Ball (Javier Ambrossi and Javier Calvo)** — Cannes Best Director for Los Javis: a three-part operatic reimagining of Lorca's unfinished play about Spain's persecution of queer people across 1932, 1937 and 2017, with Penélope Cruz and Glenn Close, Pedro Almodóvar producing. Elisabet Cabeza says it "displays the colourful and baroque intricacies of a Fabergé egg" and is "at its most powerful when Los Javis let go and embrace their story". UK and Irish cinemas since 6 Nov (Altitude); US cinemas since October; Netflix worldwide from 2 Dec.
  Read/Watch: [Elisabet Cabeza's Sight and Sound review](https://www.bfi.org.uk/sight-and-sound/reviews/black-ball-operatic-journey-through-spains-queer-histories)
- **Fjord (Cristian Mungiu)** — The Romanian director's Norway-set moral drama: a teacher reports bruises on a child, and a Christian immigrant family (Sebastian Stan and Renate Reinsve) goes up against the state's child protection services. Reviews are split, which is the interest. Sophie Monks Kaufman finds it "filled with spectacular and unnerving imagery, but let down by unconvincing characterisation", and argues that "Mungiu drains Fjord down to basic principles". Worth seeing to pick a side. UK cinemas from today; in US cinemas since October (Neon).
  Read/Watch: [Sophie Monks Kaufman's Sight and Sound review](https://www.bfi.org.uk/sight-and-sound/reviews/fjord-curiously-underdeveloped-addition-cristian-mungius-body-work-explores-religious-persecution-small-scandinavian-town)
- **Josephine (Beth de Araújo)** — Sundance's Grand Jury and Audience Award winner, from the director of *Soft & Quiet*: a crime seen through the eyes of a child, with Channing Tatum and Gemma Chan as the parents and a much-praised debut from Mason Reeves. Matt Donato calls it "the powerhouse knockout of the 2026 Sundance Film Festival", "a special, hard-to-stomach yet immaculately handled movie that demands your attention". Difficult subject matter, handled with care. US cinemas from 20 Nov; UK cinemas also from 20 Nov after its London Film Festival run.
  Read/Watch: [Matt Donato's review for TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/creative-content/reviews/josephine-review-beth-de-araujos-fearless-crime-drama-will-make-your-blood-boil-and-it-should/)
